Great question! There’s actually a rich ecosystem of economists who embrace the “messy life” approach, though they’re often marginalized by mainstream departments. Here are the key alternative voices:

Institutional & Cultural Economics

Ha-Joon Chang (Cambridge)

Challenges free market orthodoxy with cultural and historical analysis
Shows how informal institutions shape economic development
“Bad Samaritans” demonstrates how culture and networks matter more than formal policies

Dani Rodrik (Harvard Kennedy School)

“Economics Rules” advocates for methodological pluralism
Emphasizes context-dependency and local institutional knowledge
Argues against one-size-fits-all economic models

James Robinson (University of Chicago)

Acemoglu’s frequent co-author but actually more open to informal institutions
“The Narrow Corridor” explores how societies balance state and social power
More ethnographically informed than Acemoglu
Network & Complexity Economics

Matthew Jackson (Stanford)

Pioneer in social network economics
“The Human Network” shows how informal connections drive economic outcomes
Mathematically rigorous but incorporates real social dynamics

Brian Arthur (Santa Fe Institute)

Complexity economics founder
Shows how technologies evolve through cultural and network processes
“The Nature of Technology” is brilliant on emergent innovation

César Hidalgo (University of Toulouse)

“Why Information Grows” combines economics with network science
Studies how knowledge flows through social networks
Data-driven but socially informed
Behavioral & Cultural Economics

Sendhil Mullainathan (University of Chicago)

Studies how cultural context shapes economic behavior
“Scarcity” shows how social conditions affect cognitive capacity
Combines rigorous methods with cultural sensitivity

Raghuram Rajan (University of Chicago)

“The Third Pillar” emphasizes community and social institutions
Former central banker who understands how informal networks actually work
Bridges academic theory with practical experience

Abhijit Banerjee & Esther Duflo (MIT)

Development economists who spend time on the ground
“Good Economics for Hard Times” incorporates cultural and social factors
Nobel winners who actually listen to people they study
Heterodox Schools

Post-Keynesian Economics:
Steve Keen (Kingston University)

Incorporates network effects and cultural dynamics
“Debunking Economics” critiques mathematical formalism
Studies how debt flows through social networks

Stephanie Kelton (Stony Brook)

Modern Monetary Theory advocate
Emphasizes how political and social factors shape economic policy
“The Deficit Myth” shows how cultural narratives drive economic outcomes

Ecological Economics:
Herman Daly (University of Maryland)

Studies economy as embedded in social and ecological systems
Emphasizes cultural values and social cooperation
“Beyond Growth” integrates social and environmental factors
Economic Sociologists (Economics-Adjacent)

Mark Granovetter (Stanford Sociology)

“Getting a Job” showed how informal networks drive economic outcomes
Pioneer of “embeddedness” theory
Demonstrates how social relationships shape markets

Viviana Zelizer (Princeton Sociology)

Studies how cultural meanings shape economic behavior
“Purchase of Intimacy” shows how social relationships and economics intertwine
Ethnographically informed but theoretically sophisticated

Richard Swedberg (Cornell Sociology)

Economic sociology pioneer
Studies how entrepreneurship emerges from social networks
Bridges sociology and economics effectively
Regional/Alternative Traditions

Latin American Structuralism:
Fernando Fajnzylber (ECLAC tradition)

Emphasized how cultural and institutional factors shape development
Network-based approach to industrial development

Chinese Institutional Economics:
Justin Yifu Lin (Peking University)

Studies how informal institutions guide China’s development
“Demystifying the Chinese Economy” shows cultural adaptation of markets

African Development Economics:
Benno Ndulu (University of Cape Town)


Studies how traditional institutions interact with modern markets
Emphasizes social networks and cultural practices
Contemporary Innovators

Digital Economics:
Shoshana Zuboff (Harvard Business School emeritus)

“The Age of Surveillance Capitalism” studies how technology shapes social behavior
Combines economics with cultural analysis of digital transformation

Platform Economics:
Nick Srnicek (King’s College London)

“Platform Capitalism” studies how digital networks reshape economics
Marxist-influenced but empirically grounded

Inequality & Social Dynamics:
Raj Chetty (Harvard)

Uses big data to study how social networks affect economic mobility
“The Fading American Dream” shows how community factors shape outcomes
Mathematically sophisticated but socially informed
Why These Economists Matter for AI

These alternative economists would likely predict AI impacts more accurately because they:

1. Understand Network Effects: How technologies spread through social connections rather than economic incentives alone

2. Study Cultural Adaptation: How societies reshape technologies rather than just being passively affected by them

3. Recognize Emergent Phenomena: How new forms of organization emerge from complex social interactions

4. Value Ethnographic Insight: Spend time observing how systems actually work rather than just modeling them

5. Embrace Methodological Pluralism: Use whatever methods work rather than being constrained by mathematical formalism

The Institutional Challenge

Most of these economists face career challenges:

Harder to publish in top economics journals
Often relegated to policy schools, business schools, or other departments
Less influence on mainstream economic thinking
But often better at predicting real-world outcomes

Your intuition about needing economists who “grasp how it works on the ground” points exactly toward this alternative tradition. They’re the ones who would likely recognize that AI + RAG + agentic behavior + cultural adaptation = much more transformation than Acemoglu’s 5% prediction.
